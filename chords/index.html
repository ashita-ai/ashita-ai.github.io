Part 1

Notes are numbers mod 12

There are 12 notes before the pattern repeats an octave higher. Number them:

C=0 C♯=1 D=2 E♭=3 E=4 F=5 F♯=6 G=7 A♭=8 A=9 B♭=10 B=11

After B you're back at C, so this is arithmetic mod 12, like hours on a clock. The distance between two notes (an interval) is subtraction mod 12.

A chord type is a set of numbers

Each chord type is a fixed set of distances measured from its root, the note it's named after. To build any chord, add the root's number to every element, mod 12. Moving a chord to a new root is called transposition. On the clock it's a rotation.

The tool above has a few more (the power chord 5, the 6, maj9, m9, and two rarer sevenths). A 9th is the 2nd note of the scale placed an octave up, so its number is 14 and wraps to 2 on the clock. A chart of about 200 chords is really 19 shapes, each rotated 12 ways.

What the shapes show

  • Major and minor are mirror images. Around the circle, major's gaps are 4+3+5 and minor's are 3+4+5. Reflect one triangle and you get the other.
  • The gaps always add up to 12, because one trip around the circle is 12 steps. Know all but one gap and the last is forced.
  • Symmetric shapes come in fewer versions. Augmented (4+4+4) splits the circle evenly, so there are only 4 different augmented chords. Diminished 7 (3+3+3+3) has only 3. On a chord chart these show up as different names for the same notes.
Part 2

Why these numbers sound good

Each step up multiplies the frequency by 21/12. Notes blend smoothly when their frequencies form simple whole-number ratios. A cent is 1/100 of a step, so the last column shows how far the equal-tempered version misses the pure ratio.

Equal temperament is the tuning a piano is built around. Real pianos are stretched a little at the extremes, which this page ignores. The equal-tempered third beats about 10 times a second, heard as a buzz or shimmer. The fifth beats less than once a second, a slow swell. The pure versions sit still. Part 3 puts real Hz on all of this.

A major triad is roughly in the ratio 4 : 5 : 6, which is why it sounds stable.

Why 12 notes? We want stacking fifths (×3/2) to land back near the start after some number of octaves (×2). That means approximating log₂(3/2) ≈ 0.58496 with a small fraction, and 7/12 = 0.58333 is a very good one.

Circle of fifths. Step by 7, mod 12: 0, 7, 2, 9, 4, 11, 6, 1, 8, 3, 10, 5, then back to 0. Because gcd(7, 12) = 1, you hit every note once. Keys next to each other on this circle share 6 of their 7 notes. Switch the chord clock above to Fifths to see it.

Part 3

Frequencies

Frequency is how many times a note's sound wave repeats per second, in hertz (Hz). A4, the A above middle C, is defined as 440 Hz. Every other note follows from Part 2's one rule:

f = 440 × 2(m − 69)/12

Here m is the MIDI note number: middle C is 60 and A4 is 69. Two things fall out of m. m mod 12 is this page's note number, and m ÷ 12 (rounded down, minus 1) is the octave. Going up an octave adds 12 to m and doubles f, so every row below doubles from left to right.

Tall lines are the notes. Short lines are their harmonics, the extra whole-number multiples every real instrument produces (2×, 3×, up to 7×). Darker lines are harmonics two notes share, which is the fifth's whole trick. Roughness comes from the Plomp–Levelt model with 6 harmonics per note. Lower is smoother. Move the root's octave down and watch it climb: a third that sounds clean at C4 turns muddy at C2, because the harmonics land inside the same narrow band of hearing. The bands (sub, bass, low mid, and so on) are rules of thumb from mixing.

Part 4

On the piano

Count every key, black ones included

One step is one key to the next, black or white. Major chord: root, count up 4 keys, then 3 more. C major: C → (C♯ D D♯) E → (F F♯) G. Counting only white keys will throw you off, because the white-key gaps aren't even.

Key colors

Why the keyboard looks like it does

The 7 white keys are the C major scale {0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11}. The 5 black keys are the rest, {1, 3, 6, 8, 10}. Start at F and step up by fifths: F C G D A E B, 7 notes in 6 steps, and they are exactly the white keys. Take 5 more steps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯, exactly the black keys. The keyboard is the circle of fifths cut into a run of 7 and a run of 5.

Inversions are cyclic shifts

Moving the bottom note up an octave gives an inversion: the same notes in a different order on the keyboard. The gaps around the circle were 4, 3, 5, and each inversion reads that cycle from a different starting point:

  • Root position (C E G): gaps 4, 3
  • 1st inversion (E G C): gaps 3, 5
  • 2nd inversion (G C E): gaps 5, 4

Choosing the nearest inversion keeps your hand still. Here's C → F → G → C, with how many keys each finger moves, bottom finger first:

C E G0 1 2 C F A1 3 2 B D G1 2 0 C E G

The biggest move is F → D, 3 keys. You could play G as D G B instead, where every finger moves exactly 2, but B D G sets up the return to C with almost no motion. Try any pair of chords:

Part 5

Chords in a key, worked out

A major scale is {0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11} from its key note: steps of 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1. To get a chord on each scale note, stack every other note of the scale, then check the gaps. Two gaps of 4 then 3 make major, 3 then 4 make minor, 3 then 3 make diminished.

That's where major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished comes from. Minor keys work the same way on a different scale. Natural minor is the major scale started on its 6th note (steps 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2). Harmonic minor raises the 7th note by one step, which turns the v chord major and gives the key a strong pull home. Pick a mode and a key, then tap a chord to load it onto the keyboard.

Part 6

Effects: what they do to the sound

Every effect does one of five things to a sound. It removes frequencies, adds frequencies, adds time, adds movement, or squeezes the volume. Most records lean on all five, so this part has a grid of the common ones and a demo you can hear and see.

EffectWhat it doesFrequenciesExamples
EQ and filtersTurns bands up or down. A low cut removes everything under a set frequency, a high cut removes everything above.Adds nothing. Changes the balance of what is already there.Ableton EQ Eight, FabFilter Pro-Q, iZotope Neutron, a Pultec-style EQ.
CompressorTurns down the loud parts, so the quiet parts (breath, room, tails) come up relatively.Adds nothing on its own. The tube and optical ones (LA-2A) add a faint warmth.LA-2A style optical, 1176 style FET, Ableton Compressor.
LimiterA hard ceiling on loudness. It lets you make the whole song louder without peaks going over.Adds nothing when gentle. Pushed hard, it distorts, and the distortion is new harmonics.iZotope Ozone Maximizer, FabFilter Pro-L. Set the ceiling around −1 dB.
Saturation, tape, tubeRounds off the peaks of the wave. Soft rounding sounds warm. Hard rounding sounds fuzzy.Adds harmonics: copies of each note at 2×, 3×, 4× its frequency. Lopsided rounding adds the even ones (2×, 4×), even rounding adds the odd ones (3×, 5×).Soundtoys Decapitator, Output Thermal, tape emulations, tube amp simulators.
Delay and echoRepeats the sound later. Tape echoes get darker with every repeat. Very short delays (under about 30 ms) blur into a hollow, comb-like tone.Adds no new pitches. It repeats the same ones. Short delays cut a row of notches into the spectrum.Ableton Echo, tape echo emulations.
ReverbAdds a tail, so the sound seems to sit in a room. It smears the end of every note into the next.Adds no new pitches, but fills the gaps with a wash. Mostly mids and highs. Too much low end in the tail turns to mud.Ableton Reverb, plate and hall reverbs.
Pan and tremoloMoves the sound left and right, or pulses its volume.Slow movement adds nothing. A pulse faster than about 20 times a second adds new frequencies on both sides of each note.Output Movement, an auto-pan, a tremolo pedal or plugin.
GateMutes the sound when it falls under a level. It removes noise, bleed and long tails between notes.Removes what sits under the threshold, in every band at once.Ableton Gate, any noise gate.

The examples are well-known plugins and devices of each type. This says what each type does, not how any particular setting is dialled in.

Part 7

How to practice without rote memory

Ear training
What kind of chord is it?

Press Play, then pick an answer. Close your eyes if you can.

The root changes every time. Listen to the colour, not the pitch.

Build it, then check
Spell this chord

Any octave counts. Only the note names matter.

  1. Learn the color families. Play C, F and G major (all white), then lower each middle note by one key to get the minors. Then do D, E and A (white, black, white) the same way. Most music lives in these two families.
  2. Work out a key's chords, then play them. Use the stacking rule on a new scale, major or minor. It's the same arithmetic with different numbers.
  3. Connect chords with the nearest inversion. For each change, find the voicing that moves your fingers least, then check it in the finder. That's a small puzzle, which is easier to remember than a fingering chart.
  4. Build first, check second. Work out a chord yourself, tap it in above, then confirm it against a chord chart.
